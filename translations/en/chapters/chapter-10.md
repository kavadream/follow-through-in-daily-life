# Chapter 10: Where to Return After an Interruption

Lin Yue read regularly for a while, then an unexpected family matter kept her away from the book for two weeks. Once it was over, restarting felt harder than beginning the first time. The book was unchanged and the bookmark still there, but she felt she owed a great many pages. Reading, once relaxed, had become something requiring compensation.

She decided to start again the following month, when the record would look tidier. A two-week interruption became a longer wait.

When returning, the most pressing issue is often not just the task but the meaning you have given the interruption. If one absence means that all previous effort was wasted, the cost of returning rises sharply.

## First See What Is Actually Still There

An interruption does not automatically erase completed work. Drafts remain, organized photographs are still usable, and what you learned need not all be denied because of a gap. Before resuming, check the current state rather than rushing to rebuild the entire plan.

Lin Yue looked at her bookmark and notes. She remembered the main question and had forgotten only a few details. She did not need to reread from page one, only glance at the end of the previous section. Zhou Hang opened his practice file and tried a sort to see which steps he still knew and which needed looking up again.

In a real-world study, Lally and colleagues observed individual variation in the development of behavioral automaticity. Missing one opportunity did not materially disrupt the formation process in that study. The study involved particular everyday behaviors and self-reported data. It cannot guarantee that a long interruption has no effect, or establish that everyone should form a habit on the same day. [Research source](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)

This book therefore makes no universal promise about how many days a habit takes to form, and does not treat one blank day as resetting everything to zero. What matters is what you can still use now and how to reconnect next time.

## Do Not Begin by Paying an Imaginary Debt

If you planned to read ten pages a day and stopped for fourteen days, must you first make up 140 pages? Recalculating the total is necessary only when it corresponds to a real deadline and required work. For reading you have chosen yourself, adding every past gap to a debt often makes returning harder.

You can begin with a manageable section without first proving you deserve to return. If there is an actual exam, delivery date, or other deadline, honestly adjust scope or scheduling or seek help, rather than concealing an unrealistic overall plan behind “getting back into the routine.”

After interrupting her album work, Chen Jing did not schedule a whole day to catch up. She first spent a short period checking file locations and finding the next stack of photographs, then left a clear starting point. This return step was smaller than an ordinary session because it also had to reacquaint her with the project.

## Take Responsibility for Mistakes Without Humiliating Yourself

Sometimes an interruption does involve choices you can change. You may have spent all the available time on unrelated browsing, or failed to save a file despite knowing you should. Acknowledging these actions does not require the further conclusion that you are incapable as a person.

Experiments by Breines and Chen found that, in the failure or personal-weakness situations studied, participants in self-compassion conditions showed stronger scores on several measures of motivation for self-improvement. One experiment observed more subsequent study time. This does not prove that treating yourself gently solves all procrastination, but it does suggest that berating yourself is not a necessary condition for improvement. [Research source](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22645164/)

In practice, you might write: “This time I did not protect the period I had set aside. Next time, I will finish this small section before opening other pages.” That acknowledges the facts and offers a change. “I can never stick with anything” both overstates the conclusion and offers no next step.

## When an Attempt to Return Does Not Work

Lin Yue once decided to resume reading at lunchtime on Wednesday. She opened the book and read two paragraphs, then took an unexpected call and did not return before the break ended. She was tempted to count this as another failure, proof that she could not even restart properly.

A closer look showed something more specific. She had found the book, knew where to resume, and had actually started reading. She had not continued because the chosen period was interrupted. Next time, she might need to change the opportunity rather than shrink the reading goal to a single word.

On another occasion, she had an uninterrupted gap but wanted to turn away as soon as she opened the book. Thinking it over, she realized she was no longer interested in the book and only wanted to repair the broken record. The issue now belonged to choosing a goal, not adding more techniques for restarting.

Returning involves trial and error too. An attempt gives you more accurate information. Do not erase the connection you have restored just because you have not immediately regained the old frequency. Equally, starting once does not mean every condition is now in place.

If an arrangement repeatedly meets the same difficulty, change at least the relevant condition next time. Keeping the same demanding plan and merely choosing a more determined date rarely changes the circumstances by itself.

## Revise Plans for Recurring Interruptions

If every business trip stops a plan, stop treating each trip as an exception. If evening work often runs late, stop pretending the original time is dependable. Recurring interruptions are information that the arrangement needs to change.

You can prepare portable materials, use a more flexible frequency, or explicitly pause during certain periods. Preserving the current state and the conditions for resuming is often more honest than repeatedly declaring “business as usual.”

If you have an interrupted project today, first inspect its state: what has been completed, what is the smallest action that reconnects you, and which conditions of the original plan no longer hold? Then choose a specific opportunity to return.

You need not wait for guilt to disappear, or suppress it with one excessive burst of work. Returning can happen quietly: open the right file, find where you stopped, and continue with the section in front of you.
