# Chapter 2: Give What You Want to Do a Real Place

Chen Jing once made a weekend list: read, practice speaking a foreign language, organize photographs, learn to bake bread, and clear out files. None of these was unreasonable on its own. Together, they required a weekend without housework, fatigue, or surprises. By Sunday evening she had done the shopping and cared for her family, while her personal list was almost untouched.

She moved the unfinished items to the next week. Each time she carried the list forward, it grew longer, until it began to resemble a statement of debt.

Many plans run into trouble before they begin. They describe what you want, but not what it will use up. Time does not become available just because something appears on a list. Wanting to do one thing also requires allowing yourself to do less of something else.

## Distinguish Wishes to Keep from Projects in Progress

Some wishes are worth keeping without starting them now. Chen Jing still wanted to learn to bake bread, but what mattered most at the moment was turning the photographs her mother had left into an album people could browse. She moved bread baking, language practice, and old files to a “Consider Later” page, and reserved two specific periods for the album.

This was not a permanent ranking of her life. It simply acknowledged that, for the near future, some activities would receive actual resources while others remained possibilities. Without that distinction, every good idea can become a new obligation that no one has stopped to consider.

An active project should have a near-term result you can name. For the album, that might be “Choose twenty photographs with date captions,” rather than “Preserve family memories well.” The latter matters, but cannot tell you what you accomplished in an hour on Saturday.

Some activities have no final product, such as reading a little each day or keeping a room reasonably tidy. For these, you can define a maintenance standard: everyday objects are easy to find, the dining table is usable, and reading stays connected to questions you care about. Maintenance need not disguise itself as a project that grows forever.

## Estimate Capacity from the Life You Have Already Lived

Rather than asking how much time an ideal week contains, look back at the past week. Which periods were genuinely yours to use? Which suited thinking, and which suited only simple tasks? If unexpected work often takes over the evenings, do not place everything important there simply because you wish to.

Chen Jing had intended to spend three hours organizing on Saturday afternoon. Looking at her actual commitments, she realized that she often needed to drive family members around then. The more reliable time was forty minutes after Saturday breakfast. Forty minutes could not finish the album, but it could handle a small stack of photographs. A plan placed in space you actually have is better than one placed in a vast blank space that does not exist.

Leave room for finishing up, too. Cooking includes cleaning the counter; painting includes putting away materials; organizing photographs includes saving files somewhere clearly identified. Counting only the main activity turns cleanup into an obstacle before the next start.

A buffer has a practical meaning as well. If a period already contains forty minutes of work, you need not squeeze another task into its last five minutes. Answering the door, getting water, and finding files do not disappear because your plan is tightly packed.

## Do Not Requisition Every Free Moment

Sometimes, as soon as we find a spare half hour, we decide it must be used for self-improvement. But free time can also make room for rest, a walk, doing nothing, or being with family. If every gap must prove its value, a personal project can become another job.

You can take a wish seriously without giving it all your remaining energy. Especially when life is demanding, “once a week” may be more honest than “every day without fail.” Frequency should serve the activity: learning needs review, filing can be done in batches, and repairing a chair has a clear endpoint. No single frequency suits every task.

Acknowledge the cost of choosing. Giving time to the album meant that Chen Jing would not organize every cupboard the way she wanted that month. She stopped blaming herself for not organizing cupboards while she worked on photographs. A choice already made need not go on trial again every time you act on it.

## When Everything Claims to Be Important

Sometimes everything on the list really does matter and cannot easily be removed. Learning a skill for work, dealing with family documents, and maintaining a living space all have practical reasons behind them. Start by distinguishing tasks with clear consequences and deadlines from long-term wishes to improve life. Putting them on one list does not mean they all need to move forward this week.

For example, Zhou Hang needed to finish a work document by Friday and also wanted to learn more spreadsheet skills. Rather than launching a large separate learning plan, he first learned the operations this document required. Unrelated techniques could wait. The limitation was clear: this met an immediate need without necessarily building a complete body of knowledge. Once the immediate pressure eased, he could decide whether to add systematic study.

Some burdens cannot be absorbed through personal optimization. If a new task requires an old one to give way, but you have no authority to change the workload, you need to explain the capacity conflict. Do not assign two incompatible tasks to the same hour on paper and leave your future self to bear the conflict.

The same applies to personal projects. You need not wait until you are completely free to begin, but you do need to acknowledge what each new commitment uses. Sometimes the most important line in a workable plan is, “For now, I am not doing this.”

## Write a Plan with Limits

Choose one project you want to move forward. Write down a near-term result, the time available, and what you will not do for now. Finally, add a review date when you will decide again based on what has happened, rather than extending the arrangement indefinitely.

For example: “Over the next two weeks, choose twenty photographs and add dates where they can be confirmed. Work after breakfast on Saturdays, for no more than forty minutes. Do not design a cover or buy new equipment yet. After two weeks, see whether this arrangement works.” The numbers are examples. You can make them smaller, or larger if your circumstances allow.

If you cannot identify any available time, do not add more reminders yet. The problem you need to address is capacity. Reducing projects, postponing a start, or finding support is more useful than making more wishes for an already crowded week.

Once what you want to do has a real place, you can turn to how to enter it.
