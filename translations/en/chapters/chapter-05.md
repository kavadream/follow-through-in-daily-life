# Chapter 5: Plan Work Around the Capacity You Have Today

For a while, Lin Yue set aside bedtime for reading. She thought the quiet of the night made it the best time for her own interests. But when it arrived, she often no longer wanted to work through complicated sentences. She struggled through a few pages and could not remember them the next day, so she added another rule: she must take notes.

The burden grew, while the time remained the same. Her attitude toward reading might not have been the problem. She had placed an activity requiring clear thought in a period when she was already tired.

This chapter does not compare human energy to a battery of fixed capacity or offer a physiological explanation of depleted willpower. We start with what can be observed: the same person may be able to handle different levels of difficulty on different days and at different times. Plans need to accommodate that variation.

## Distinguish What Tasks Ask of You

Activities that all look like “working on a project” can make very different demands. Designing an album's structure requires decisions. Adding confirmed dates to selected photographs is largely a matter of checking. Making a backup copy is relatively mechanical. All three matter, but they need not compete for the same kind of time.

Whenever Chen Jing sat down, she used to insist on first deciding the style of the entire album. The thought of all those choices made her retreat. Later, she separated the work: when she felt clearer, she decided sections and order; on ordinary evenings, she checked a few photographs; during particularly busy weeks, she simply kept completed materials safe without adding more work.

This allocation does not mean you must fill tired moments with easy tasks. Rest can be tonight's plan. If even simple work feels strenuous, cutting the task into still smaller pieces may not be the best choice.

## Keep Different Versions for Different Days

A plan with only two options, “complete everything” and “fail completely,” struggles with ordinary life. You can prepare versions of different sizes for the same activity, provided you name each honestly.

Lin Yue's usual reading meant finishing a short section and thinking about its question. When busy, she only reviewed her last brief note and decided where to resume. When she needed rest, she did not read, and did not count glancing at the cover as reading. The smaller version kept a connection alive; it did not amount to a full learning session.

The distinction matters. Giving every situation the same completion mark makes a record less informative. Requiring every day to meet the same standard turns difficult days into a string of failures. You can acknowledge both that you did less today and that this small amount served a purpose.

Be careful, too, that the small version does not conceal a lasting lack of capacity. If you have managed only preparatory actions for several weeks, revisit the project plan rather than endlessly expecting things to “return to normal tomorrow.” You may need another time, fewer simultaneous activities, or acceptance that this stage of life calls for a pause.

## Easy Work Can Also Become Avoidance

Once you assign tasks according to available capacity, watch for another bias: always choosing the easiest task, regardless of how you feel. Zhou Hang spent several sessions organizing course contents, saying it was not a good day for thinking. Looking back, he realized that on one occasion he had been quite alert. Fear of making mistakes in practice had sent him to organize materials instead.

There is no need to abandon the whole approach. Identify the actual difficulty again. If you are tired, reduce the burden. If you fear mistakes, use a copy for a low-risk exercise. If you do not know the next step, write out the problem clearly. Different causes cannot forever be explained by “I'm not at my best today.”

Conversely, do not treat every need for rest as suspected avoidance. You need not prove that you are completely exhausted before you are entitled to stop. You can look at recent attempts to see whether rest makes returning easier or whether a particular time is repeatedly unsuitable, without interrogating your motives every time.

Chen Jing later began handling one task requiring judgment on a weekend morning, then arranging mechanical checking afterward. She did not try to exploit every alert minute. She simply wanted important decisions to stop being pushed into her most exhausted evenings. The change finally gave the harder work a place while allowing the lighter version to keep serving its original purpose.

## Give Work an Ending Boundary

Sometimes the difficulty is not starting but refusing to stop. When Zhou Hang had an unusually good study session, he worked deep into the night. The next day he was tired and did not want to touch the computer that evening. A few days later, another long burst made up for it. This rhythm increasingly tied learning to overextending himself.

Before starting, he began choosing a rough stopping point: finish this set of exercises, save the questions, then decide whether to continue. Continuing was possible, but he could not keep treating the next day's arrangements as cost-free. A good day need not drain the next several sessions in advance.

An ending boundary also protects other everyday needs. You may still need to cook, care for family, sleep, or simply sit quietly for a while. The importance of a personal project does not cancel those needs.

## Practice Scaling Honestly

Write three versions of your current activity: what you do under ordinary conditions, what you preserve when time is limited, and when you simply rest. Do not call them excellent, passing, and failing. They are arrangements for different conditions.

Just before acting, choose according to today's circumstances rather than yesterday's ambition. At the end, write one sentence about whether the version suited you. If you consistently overestimate, lower the next plan a little. If you often have room left, increase it gradually.

This book is not intended to diagnose or treat persistent fatigue, attention difficulties, or other health problems. Do not substitute follow-through exercises for a professional assessment you need, or ignore how your body feels to fit the book's arrangements. The aim is to make everyday plans more responsive to real life, not to require your body to obey a plan.
