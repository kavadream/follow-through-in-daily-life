# Chapter 8: See Progress Without Being Led by Numbers

Zhou Hang began recording study time. He liked watching the weekly figure rise, so he counted time spent searching for tutorials, organizing files, and playing videos. A month later, the record looked excellent, but he still could not work through the spreadsheet independently.

Records can help us judge what is happening, or offer satisfaction disconnected from real results. The problem is not the numbers themselves, but whether they represent what actually matters to you.

## First Decide Which Decision the Record Should Help You Make

If you want to know whether you have found usable time, record how often you actually begin. If you want to know whether a skill is improving, look at what you can do independently. If you want to know how far a project is from completion, record finished parts and remaining obstacles.

One measure cannot answer every question. Pages read describe volume, not necessarily understanding. The number of objects organized does not show whether a home is easier to use. A streak of days does not show whether a goal remains worth pursuing.

A meta-analysis of experimental studies by Harkin and colleagues found that interventions encouraging monitoring of goal progress helped goal attainment on average. This supports the value of making progress visible. It does not mean that more detailed records are always better, or that everyone must post public check-ins. [Research source](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/)

This book suggests beginning with a simple record that supports a decision rather than a dashboard requiring long-term upkeep. Research offers a direction; the task and your circumstances still determine what to record.

## Look at Both Actions and Results

Zhou Hang kept recording study sessions but added something more direct: “Could I complete the sort independently this time, and where did I need to look something up?” After two entries, he noticed that he always made a mistake at the same point. Next time he practiced that specific step rather than continuing to a new lesson.

For her album, Chen Jing recorded “photographs with confirmed captions” instead of “minutes at the computer.” She also kept time information, however, because if organizing ten photos took far longer than expected, she would need to adjust the project's scope. Records of actions and records of results each have uses; one need not replace the other.

A record can include noncompletion too. “Unexpected overtime; did not begin” tells you more than a blank box. “Started, then found information missing” guides the next step better than a red cross. You do not need a long explanation for every gap; a word or two is enough.

## Do Not Let Rewards Change What You Set Out to Do

Lin Yue once joined a reading check-in activity. Later, she noticed she was choosing short, easy books because they increased her count. She had wanted to understand a complex problem, but was beginning to avoid the longer books most relevant to it.

When a measure redirects your choices, reconsider the measure. You can keep recording pages without treating them as the only score. Or you can change the goal to “Understand two explanations of this problem,” recognizing that some slow-reading weeks are valuable.

Public records also have costs. Some people gain support; others begin performing; still others do not want to expose their personal lives. Whether to go public should be your choice. You need not trade privacy for follow-through. If you involve a partner, share only what is needed to get help and agree on how they will support you, rather than assuming you must accept supervision and blame.

## When Numbers Fall, First Look at What Happened

One week, Zhou Hang's practice sessions fell from three to two. He was about to add tasks to the next week to make up for it when he noticed that one of those two sessions had completed an operation he had previously been unable to do, without consulting a tutorial. The count fell while his independent ability improved. A single measure would have misclassified the week as a setback.

By contrast, one week he practiced four times, each time copying the same familiar example. The count rose without addressing a new difficulty. This did not mean those four sessions were useless, only that they could not support a claim of clear improvement in ability.

When reviewing records, first identify what changed. Work volume, difficulty, available time, and evaluation methods may all differ. A fall in output is unsurprising when you take on harder tasks. If the task is unchanged but difficulty keeps increasing, examine your methods and conditions.

Do not change counting rules without explanation just to make a trend look good. If you initially counted only reading and later include buying books, even high numbers are no longer comparable. When a standard needs changing, state the change and continue from a new baseline. An honest but unattractive record is more useful than an ever-rising curve.

## Replace Daily Judgment with a Brief Review

Asking “Have I improved?” every day may be too frequent. A work in progress or a skill needing repeated practice will not necessarily show a daily change. Review at an interval appropriate to the project: what genuinely advanced, where you remain stuck, and whether the next arrangement should change.

Review need not always search for problems. A surprisingly useful time slot, a division of work that reduced searching, or stopping promptly enough to avoid postponing cleanup are also worth remembering. Records should support choices, not build a more precise system of self-reproach.

For your current project, choose one piece of action information and one piece of result information, such as “Actually began twice” and “Can independently handle three types of date input.” After recording them for a short period, ask whether this information has helped you make better decisions. If not, simplify or change the record.

When recording becomes more absorbing than the work itself, you can pause it too. A project does not have to keep producing statistics to prove it exists.
