# Chapter 3: Make the First Step Something Your Hands Can Do

Zhou Hang wanted to learn some spreadsheet skills. Every time he opened a course, he began by browsing the contents, comparing instructors, and looking up other people's recommended learning paths. Half an hour later, he knew more ways to study but had not worked on a single row of data. Closing the computer, he felt he had made an effort, yet sensed that something was wrong.

“Learn spreadsheets” is a direction, not an action your hands can carry out. Keep the direction, but give yourself a closer entry point: open the practice file, change three date entries to the same format, and see whether anything goes wrong.

## Small Actions Must Still Touch the Real Work

To make starting easier, some people shrink the action until simply opening the software counts as completion. This may help them become familiar with the entry point. But if they stay there, the project does not advance. A small action needs contact with the actual work.

Reading might mean finishing one meaningful paragraph and saying what question it answers. Writing might mean producing an explanation that is not yet elegant. Organizing might mean deciding where one object belongs. These actions can be brief, but each changes something. Buying stationery, choosing apps, and changing folder colors should not become lasting substitutes for the work itself, unless they address the obstacle currently in the way.

Zhou Hang changed his next study session to: “Open the practice file, sort the date column once, and note what I do not understand.” He still needed a tutorial, but now he would search with a particular question instead of first collecting a perfect learning system.

A small action is not a lifetime quota. If you have the capacity, you can continue. If you do not, you can stop at the boundary you set beforehand. The important thing is not to secretly turn the entry point into a trap: saying you will do only a little, then forcing yourself to work late every time. Your future self will soon learn that the promise cannot be trusted.

## Connect an Opportunity to an Action

Research on “implementation intentions” examines plans that specify a situation and an action in advance. An experimental study by Webb and Sheeran explored these plans and the relationships among situational cues, response links, and goal performance. It offers a direction worth trying: instead of keeping only a goal in mind, connect action to a recognizable opportunity. Results from experimental tasks do not, however, guarantee that every everyday plan will succeed. [Research source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2006.02.001)

In everyday life, you might write, “If I return to my seat after lunch and have ten free minutes, I will open the page I have already chosen.” The condition should be recognizable and the action doable. Avoid “Study when I have time,” because “having time” often needs interpreting all over again. And do not attach five actions to one situation, turning every opportunity into an elaborate ritual.

People with unstable schedules can use an event as an entry point, but events can disappear too. Business travel may mean no regular dining table; shift work may mean no shared evening routine. A plan needs to allow for this. If the main opportunity does not appear, wait for the next real opportunity rather than adding alarm after alarm.

The “if” here is no spell for controlling life. It simply saves you one decision in the moment. An urgent matter can take priority over the planned action. Where safety or caring for another person is involved, you certainly should not force the activity just to preserve a record.

## Put a Stepping Stone Before the Difficult Part

Some tasks have a clear first step that you still do not want to approach. Writing application materials may make you want to escape the prospect of judgment. Making mistakes while learning a new skill may leave you feeling foolish. Shortening the session alone may not be enough; you also need to reduce the pressure to be evaluated on your first attempt.

You can begin with a rough draft for your eyes only, an unimportant practice file, or a sketch you are free to erase. Do not confuse a low-consequence practice attempt with a final version you must deliver. A trial is meant to give you information for the next step, not to prove your ability immediately.

The first time Zhou Hang sorted the dates, he put them in the wrong order. Previously, he would have quit at once, deciding he was not suited to the work. This time he was using a copy, so the mistake did not damage his work file. He noted, “Some dates seem to be text.” That became the next problem to solve. Starting no longer meant having to get it right the first time.

## Leave an Action for Next Time

Today, take one vague task and rewrite it as something your hands can do. Check three things: where the tools are, what you will work on first, and where you can stop. Then identify an entry point that might realistically arise.

If your first step is “Figure out how to do it,” keep asking: to figure it out, which material will you read, which question will you write, or which version will you try? Thinking is certainly work, but it can have a specific object too.

A genuine start need not look impressive. It only needs to leave the project recognizably different, however slightly, from when you began.
