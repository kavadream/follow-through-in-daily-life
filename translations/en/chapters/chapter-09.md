# Chapter 9: Continue After the Novelty Fades

When Chen Jing began the album, every photograph held a story. Later, she had to keep checking dates, correcting orientation, and handling duplicate files. This work was less moving than the beginning, but necessary if the album was to be usable.

For a while, she thought she had lost her passion. In fact, the project had entered another stage. Beginnings invite imagination; middles often involve repetition, waiting, and unglamorous detail. If we recognize our willingness only when we feel excited, many worthwhile things will stop halfway.

## Distinguish Boredom from Loss of Meaning

Boredom does not automatically mean the direction is wrong. You may still value the result while disliking part of the process. Or actual experience may show that the goal matters less to you than you imagined. These situations need distinguishing.

Chen Jing asked herself: if all these dates had already been checked, would I still want the album? She would. She wanted her family to know who was in each photograph and where it was taken. That answer helped her accept some uneventful work. Had the answer been no, she should have reconsidered the album's form rather than simply forcing herself to persist.

Do not demand a strong sense of meaning every time you act. Sometimes, doing something you have already judged worthwhile means following the existing arrangement for a short stretch. Meaning can be confirmed during a review; you need not feel it afresh every ten minutes.

## Divide Repetitive Work into Visible Sections

An unbounded instruction to “keep organizing” easily becomes tiresome. Chen Jing grouped the photographs by envelope and dealt with one group at a time. At the end, she knew that stack was finished, rather than facing a large box that seemed never to shrink.

Sections can be defined by object, question, or stage of work. Finishing a short reading section, dealing with a drawer, or testing one module is more recognizable than “do as much as possible.” Section size need not be fixed. What matters is a clear completion condition and not sacrificing quality for quantity.

Variety can make the process more agreeable. You can change the type of practice question, alternate kinds of photographs, or move somewhere else when possible. But do not keep changing your whole toolset and goals in pursuit of novelty. Variation should help you continue the current work, rather than keep you forever at the most exciting beginning of each project.

## Do Not Postpone All Enjoyment Until Completion

Some people forbid themselves any other enjoyable activity until the task is finished. The larger the project, the more life resembles an indefinitely extended wait. This arrangement is especially hard to sustain for a long-term project.

Chen Jing did not devote every weekend to the album. She worked within the available time, then took walks, cooked, and watched programs she liked as usual. The project still mattered, but no longer controlled her whole life. After finishing a meaningful section, you can also stop to look at what is there instead of immediately reminding yourself how much remains.

If you use rewards, keep them proportionate to the burden, harmless to your life, and free of traps that turn the task into a bargain against yourself. “After this small section, take a break” can be a gentle arrangement. “No eating or sleeping until it is done” is unnecessary. Basic needs should not become tools of punishment.

## Allow Ability to Grow More Slowly Than Enthusiasm

During spreadsheet practice, Zhou Hang had several days when he felt he was improving quickly. Then he met a more complex file and suddenly needed frequent reference checks again. He interpreted this as regression and wanted to restart with a different course.

Looking back, he saw that earlier exercises had been uniform, whereas the new file contained mixed formats. He had not forgotten everything he knew; he was facing a new level of difficulty. He kept the original course and added practice closer to the real problem.

When progress slows, first ask whether the task has become harder, the standard has changed, or you are filling in foundations. A fall in novelty alone does not prove the method ineffective. Equally, having persisted for a long time is no reason to refuse to change an approach that clearly does not work.

Today, choose one boring but necessary task in your project. State the result it serves, then define a short section you can finish. If you cannot explain its purpose, do not continue mechanically. Perhaps this item can already be removed.

The reason to continue can be quiet. You know it is useful, know which part you will handle today, and know that afterward you can return to the rest of life.
