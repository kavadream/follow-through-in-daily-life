# Chapter 13: Some Goals Need Changing, Not More Endurance

Halfway through a book, Lin Yue realized that the questions its author kept discussing were no longer the ones she cared most about. She still opened it every day, because leaving it unfinished made the time already spent feel wasted. Meanwhile, she had not started another book directly relevant to her current work.

Advice to persist can be counterproductive here. Unless you first ask whether a goal remains worth pursuing, a more effective method of following through may keep you on an unsuitable path more efficiently.

## Separate the Result from the Original Method

A goal often contains a deeper need. Wanting to read every day might mean wanting to understand a question, or wanting quiet time alone. Wanting to learn photography might mean wanting to record family life, not necessarily to master every kind of equipment systematically. Seeing the need clearly helps you judge whether the original method is still necessary.

Lin Yue wanted to understand a new concept at work. The book she had chosen was only one possible route. She switched to a more direct article and resolved the immediate question. The book could wait until later, or remain unfinished. She had not lost her learning goal; she had changed the material.

Zhou Hang's situation was different. A difficult exercise made him want to abandon spreadsheet learning. But he still needed to handle everyday data, and the difficulty centered on one learnable operation. Adjusting the practice and finding an explanation made more sense than immediately replacing the goal.

“This feels bad right now” and “This direction no longer fits” are different judgments. The first deserves attention, but cannot alone decide every question of staying or leaving. Look at where the difficulty comes from: temporary lack of skill, missing resources, changed circumstances, or a goal that no longer matters.

## Set Review Conditions When You Are Calm

If you reconsider whether to continue at every difficulty, you may never gain enough experience. If you never reconsider, you may spend effort for a long time without good reason. When starting a project, agree on a suitable review point or identify circumstances that would call for an earlier adjustment.

For example: “After three small exercises, see whether this course helps me work with actual files.” “If the equipment costs more than I can afford, change the form of the project.” “If more important caregiving responsibilities arise during this period, pause rather than cut necessary rest.” These conditions should come from real life, not a desire to appear strict.

At review time, focus on evidence from what has happened. Which parts are valuable? Which costs are higher than expected? Has changing the method helped? You need not make permanent decisions in despair, or add long-term commitments in a moment of excitement.

Wrosch and colleagues examined associations among disengaging from unattainable goals, reengaging with other goals, and subjective well-being. Their work suggests that adjusting goals can form part of self-regulation. Such associations cannot decide whether a particular project of yours should stop, and do not show that quitting whenever something is hard is necessarily better. ## Leaving Also Means Dealing with What Remains

Ending a personal project usually involves more than closing a folder. If you have made commitments to others, explain the change. If you are using shared resources, return them. If there are completed results, save them so you can find them again.

Chen Jing had once wanted to expand the album into a large family-history project. Later, she decided not to expand it and to keep only the finished short version. She archived the collected material, marked unverified information, and explained the scope to relatives who had provided photographs. The project did not fulfill its original grand vision, but left a usable result and a clear ending.

Other projects can pause rather than end. A pause needs a practical reason and a condition for reconsidering, such as obtaining relevant information, the end of a busy work season, or a specified review date. If the date simply keeps moving back, acknowledge that the project currently has no place in life instead of keeping it permanently on the daily list.

## Consider the Time Already Invested

Time spent in the past has already happened. It may have brought knowledge or experience, or genuinely failed to produce the value you hoped for. Whether further investment is worthwhile depends on what you may gain in the future and what more it will cost.

You need not feel nothing about the loss. Leaving a purchased course unfinished can be disappointing; deciding not to continue after lengthy practice can be hard. You can acknowledge those feelings without spending still more time to prove the past was not wasted.

A short closing note can sometimes help: “I tried this direction and found that my actual needs differed from the course content. Next time I choose a course, I will first look at one complete example.” That turns experience into reusable information rather than a permanent accusation against yourself.

Do not turn “knowing when to quit” into a new form of superiority, either. Some continue because the outcome still matters and circumstances allow it. Others adjust because reality has changed. Both require specific judgment; no slogan can rank one above the other.

## Make One Decision About a Stalled Project

Take something that has been hanging over you for a long time. Write down the result you still want, the future costs, and adjustments you have already tried. Then choose a current decision among continuing, reducing scope, changing methods, pausing, or ending, and write the next step.

If the evidence is insufficient, you can make a limited trial rather than immediately promise long-term persistence. Complete one real task, for example, to see whether the skill is genuinely useful. If even the trial is costly, gather the necessary information first. You need not take an excessive risk to test your interest.

What you should follow through on is what remains worth doing. Judging whether it still is worthwhile is part of the work itself.
