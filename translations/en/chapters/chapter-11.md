# Chapter 11: Big Projects Need Small Parts You Can Deliver

The number of photographs in Chen Jing's album kept growing, but the project never seemed finished. She could always think of additions: a few more travel photos, a conversation with another relative, every date checked. Her ideal album kept expanding, while even the completed parts never seemed ready to show anyone.

Large projects can trap us in two ways. One is being too large to know where to start. The other is expanding after we begin, so we never know when to stop. Breaking a project into small actions solves only the first half. The second needs clear intermediate results and boundaries for completion.

## A Small Part Should Be Usable on Its Own

“Organize for twenty minutes” is a work period, not necessarily a deliverable. “Choose, caption, and lay out five photographs so family members can browse them directly” has a recognizable result.

A small part need not prove the whole project successful, but ideally it should have an actual use or produce feedback. For a short essay, completing a paragraph a reader can understand exposes problems more readily than beautifying ten headings. For a personal website, one introduction page that opens correctly is easier to check than many unconnected pages.

Chen Jing chose to make a short version first, using only photographs whose information had been confirmed. Where dates were uncertain, she explicitly gave an approximate period rather than guessing an exact year. Information you cannot establish can remain uncertain; completeness is no reason to invent it.

## Make Dependencies Visible

Some work cannot move forward because it depends on an earlier decision or someone else's materials. Printing an album requires deciding the page count; the page count depends on choosing the range of photographs. Pushing every stage at once may generate activity without bringing completion closer.

Write down the condition currently blocking the next step and judge whether it is genuinely necessary. Some conditions can be avoided by reducing scope; others require waiting. While waiting for another person's confirmation, you can advance independent parts, but cannot make important decisions on their behalf without authorization.

In a personal data project, Zhou Hang recorded “waiting for information” separately from “I have not processed it yet.” This showed him that some parts could not be accelerated by more individual effort. He could ask once about progress, adjust the schedule, or work on something else, rather than repeatedly opening the same empty folder.

## Understand the Cost Before Adding Content

New ideas often appear midway through a project. They may genuinely improve the work, or move something nearly finished farther from its endpoint. Do not automatically include an idea in the current version as soon as it occurs.

Chen Jing put new ideas on a next-version list. She added something to the current album only if leaving it out would make that album difficult to understand or use. Decorative improvements and new themes waited. This did not deny their value; it gave the current version a chance to be finished.

Intermediate deliveries still need minimum quality standards. Reducing scope does not mean hiding known errors. A short album can contain fewer photographs, but should not misidentify people. A brief tutorial can cover only one function, but should not omit a warning needed to prevent data loss. Scope can shrink; essential truthfulness and safety requirements cannot simply shrink with it.

## Delivering Too Early Can Create Rework

Intermediate results help, but earlier delivery is not always better. Zhou Hang once sent an unchecked spreadsheet to a friend, who interpreted the data using the wrong column and gave feedback accordingly. He had wanted early input, but first had to explain which parts were only drafts.

Next time, he wrote when sharing: “Only the dates have been cleaned so far; the other columns have not been checked. This time, please look only at whether the dates display consistently.” Once the recipient knew what could be judged and what could not yet be used, the feedback had an appropriate scope.

A small version therefore needs to explain both its purpose and its limits. A draft for your own practice can tolerate considerable incompleteness. A file intended for another person's actual use must meet the relevant accuracy requirements. “Make something first” should not mean transferring every burden of judgment to the user.

The same applied to Chen Jing's short album. She could label uncertain years as approximate periods, but could not omit the uncertainty itself. She could leave out a period of family history, but should not build seemingly comprehensive conclusions on that omission. Completion boundaries let work end; stated limitations let the result be used correctly.

## Schedule the Final Steps of Finishing

Finishing a project often still involves checking, saving, naming, delivering, and archiving. If those steps are not included, the last bit of work can drag on.

Chen Jing scheduled a complete read-through of the short album, checked the captions identifying people, confirmed that the people whose photographs were used were willing to be included, and saved both an editable version and a reading version. She did not wait for every relative to provide perfect feedback, but neither did she publicly share sensitive photographs whose use remained unconfirmed.

After finishing, she noted what this version included and excluded. A next version could start there; if she did not continue for now, this version still stood as a complete, usable result.

For your current project, define the smallest version with a genuine use. State who will use it, what problem it addresses, and which checks are necessary before completion. Move ideas about how it “could be better later” somewhere else.

Following through ultimately includes allowing something to be finished. Endless improvement can be another way of delaying delivery.
