# Chapter 1: First, See Where Things Stopped

Lin Yue put a book in her commuting bag, carried it out, and brought it home again. She remembered the excitement of buying it and the date she had written on the flyleaf. Three weeks later, the bookmark was still on page nineteen. On Friday evening, she took the book out, intending to catch up with an hour of reading. Her phone sounded once, the washing machine sounded once, and by the time she sat down again, she no longer wanted to read anything.

In her notebook she wrote, “I always lose interest after the first burst of enthusiasm.” The sentence seemed to explain everything, but told her nothing about what to change tomorrow. It squeezed several different circumstances over three weeks into one personality trait. On some days she had no seat; on others the book was too difficult. One day she had read four pages, but decided that was too little to count.

This is where our book begins. Before deciding what sort of person you are, look at the precise point where things stopped. An unfinished book might be one you never opened, one you opened but could not pick up from where you left off, or one you keep reading even though its questions no longer interest you. These pauses need different responses.

## Replace Judgments with a Process You Can Observe

“I have no follow-through” is not something you can observe. “I came home, left my bag by the door, and sat on the sofa after dinner; the book stayed in the bag” is. The second description is plain, but it gives you something to change: where the book goes, whether there is a suitable seat after dinner, or whether you should choose another time to read.

You need not record your whole day or turn life into a surveillance operation. Choose one recent small thing that did not happen, and write down the few actions between preparing to begin and giving up. Use verbs where possible, rather than words about character. Instead of “I was lazy again,” write, “After opening the course page, I spent ten minutes moving between contents lists without choosing a lesson.” The problem may be too many choices, rather than too little study time.

Some pauses come from missing resources. Zhou Hang wanted to organize old family photographs, but the scanner was at a relative's home and the photos were scattered across three boxes. Every day he reminded himself, “Work on the album tonight,” and every day he felt disappointed. A reminder did not bring the scanner back. Getting the equipment, or making a trial run by photographing five prints with his phone, was something he could actually do next.

Other pauses come from unclear standards. Chen Jing wanted to “get the kitchen organized,” but opening a cupboard made the task feel enormous. She wanted to remove expired items, change the storage arrangements, and buy matching containers. When one evening had to carry three projects, it was hardly surprising that nothing felt finished. She needed to decide which task this particular session was meant to complete.

## Distinguish Not Wanting To from Not Being Able To

It is easy to interpret all inaction as not wanting something enough. But someone may genuinely want to do something and have no usable time. Someone else may have time but no longer believe in the goal. Asking only “How much do you really want it?” burdens the first person with undeserved blame while keeping the second person spending effort.

Try three concrete questions. What do you still want to gain? What exactly is the next step? What conditions are missing? The answers can be brief: “I want to understand basic information when I travel. My next step is to understand this station announcement. I need an audio recording I can replay.” That is closer to actionable work than “I want to get good at a foreign language.”

These questions also make room for uncomfortable answers. You may find that a goal survives only because you bought a course and hate to waste it, or because everyone else is doing it and you fear falling behind. Recognizing this does not require you to quit immediately. It means you need to consider whether the goal is worth continuing, rather than immediately fitting it with stricter reminders.

## Change One Obstacle at a Time

Looking back over those three weeks, Lin Yue did not draw up another annual reading plan. She noticed the most common pattern: after coming home, she could not remember the question she had last been reading about, and finding her way back felt troublesome. On her bookmark she wrote, “The next section explains why this proposal will fail.” At lunch the next day, she resumed reading from that question.

Had she not had a lunch break that day, she still could not have read. But she was no longer changing her wake-up time, phone settings, reading targets, and room layout all at once. Changing a single obstacle made it easier to see whether the change helped.

Some problems genuinely cannot be addressed with small tricks. A workload that consistently exceeds your capacity, caregiving with no one to take over, or a lack of basic safety and stability will not be resolved by a sticky note. Where you can reduce demands, seek resources, or pause personal projects, do not put those options behind every self-management technique.

## Leave One Honest Record

Today, choose something you keep wanting to do but stopping. Write three lines: when you last meant to do it, what actions actually happened, and one condition that was missing. Then choose a small, reversible adjustment to try at the next opportunity.

Do not write, “From now on, I will definitely stick with it.” Write, “Next time I have a seat during my lunch break, I will first look at the question on my bookmark.” If you still do not start next time, observe again. You are not gathering evidence of your failures. You are discovering what this activity needs.

The first step in following through is often to replace a trial of your character with a look at what actually happened.
